Reaching plants before the next shutdown or service project

A plant with its own maintenance team can still need outside help. A planned shutdown may require extra technicians. A larger repair may need a specialist. A new production line may create work the existing team cannot cover.

The campaign totals above cover 2,379 manufacturing sites, 816 decision-maker conversations and 41 booked meetings. The sections below explain an approach to conversations about planned maintenance and external service capacity.

Start with the work the provider can actually do

Agree on the service territory, supported equipment and types of work before building the calling list. Mechanical maintenance for conveyors, packaging machinery and assembly equipment may each need a different contact and a different reason to call.

Research manufacturing plants with ongoing production. Check the plant address, its production activity and signs of expansion through company websites, facility pages and business directories. Verify equipment details during the call.

Remove offices, distribution-only locations and sites outside the service area. Exclude work beyond the provider’s capabilities during qualification.

Useful contacts include maintenance managers, plant engineers and plant managers. Purchasing may join the discussion when supplier approval or a service agreement becomes relevant.

Ask about the work already on their calendar

The first call should establish who arranges external maintenance and when additional support might be needed.

Useful questions include:

  • Which equipment does your team maintain, and where do you use outside contractors?
  • Are any shutdowns or larger maintenance jobs planned?
  • Is there work your internal team struggles to cover with current staffing?
  • Are you adding or relocating production equipment?
  • How do you approve a new service provider?

Call in local business hours and use the contact’s preferred callback time when they are on the production floor. For an agreed follow-up, record the equipment, planned work and reason to call again.

An active breakdown needs a direct technical response. It should not be treated as a routine prospecting meeting.

Refine the questions as you learn

1. Find the person booking contractors. A useful routing question is “Who coordinates outside contractors for planned maintenance?” Record the correct contact for each site.

2. Explain where the provider can help. A broad introduction about maintenance services may lead to a quick answer about the internal team. Be specific about extra mechanical support for shutdowns, larger jobs and work beyond the team’s available capacity.

3. Check equipment fit early. Ask about equipment and work scope before requesting a meeting. Give the provider’s technical team enough information to check whether the opportunity belongs with them.

4. Work from the shutdown calendar. When a contact mentions a future shutdown, record when contractors will be selected. Schedule follow-up before that decision, with a reminder of the work discussed.

Review whether the handoff includes the right person, a clear service scope and work that fits the territory and technical capabilities. Use that feedback to improve the calling questions.

How to handle the common objections

“We have our own maintenance team.” Ask whether they use external technicians for shutdowns, specialist repairs or larger jobs. If everything is covered and no additional work is planned, there is no immediate reason to meet.

“We already have a contractor.” Check whether the site ever needs additional capacity or considers another approved supplier. A meeting only makes sense if the contact has a reason to explore that option.

“Nothing is coming up.” Ask when maintenance plans will next be reviewed. If the contact agrees to reconnect around that date, create a follow-up task.

“Send your capabilities.” Ask which equipment or service area they want covered. Sending the document alone does not establish a qualified opportunity.

Give the sales team the context

Before booking, confirm that the site is within the service area, the work appears to fit the provider’s capabilities, and the contact has a specific reason to meet. The next conversation can cover a planned job, an upcoming shutdown or additional supplier capacity.

The brief should record what is known and what still requires technical confirmation.

Example meeting brief

  • Site: Example site 027
  • Business: Packaging manufacturer operating multiple production shifts
  • Contact: Maintenance Manager
  • Current setup: Internal maintenance team, with outside contractors used for larger planned jobs
  • Reason for interest: An upcoming shutdown includes conveyor repairs and mechanical work the internal team may need help completing
  • Purpose of the meeting: Discuss the equipment, expected scope, shutdown window and availability of external technicians
  • Supplier process: Maintenance recommends contractors; purchasing handles approval
  • Still to confirm: Equipment details, final dates, site requirements, budget and contractor onboarding
  • Status: Booked — attendance pending

Following up before the work was assigned

The totals include 27 separate sites interested in further contact without a booked meeting. Follow-up can suit a shutdown with dates still being finalized, an expansion awaiting approval or a supplier review scheduled for later.

Each task should name the contact, the planned work and the next date to reconnect. That lets the next call pick up from the maintenance plan already discussed.

Across the campaign, 41 bookings and 27 follow-up sites add up to 68 positive responses. A useful handoff gives the provider a clear basis for its next conversation: the site, equipment, likely work and timing.
