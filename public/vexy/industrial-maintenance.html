Reaching plants before the next shutdown or service project

A plant with its own maintenance team can still need outside help. A planned shutdown may require extra technicians. A larger repair may need a specialist. A new production line may create work the existing team cannot cover.

This example follows a phone-led campaign for a US provider of production equipment maintenance. We focused on manufacturing sites within its service area and asked how they handled work that required external support.

The first filter was whether the provider could do the work

We agreed the service territory, supported equipment and types of work before building the calling list. The example campaign covers defined Midwest service areas, with mechanical maintenance for conveyors, packaging machinery and assembly equipment.

Research focused on manufacturing plants with ongoing production, including industrial components, fabricated products and packaging. We checked the plant address, its production activity and signs of expansion through company websites, facility pages and business directories. Equipment details were verified during the call.

Offices, distribution-only locations and sites outside the service area were removed. Sites needing work beyond the provider’s capabilities were excluded during qualification.

Our priority contacts were maintenance managers, plant engineers and plant managers. Purchasing joined the discussion when supplier approval or a service agreement became relevant.

We asked about the work already on their calendar

The first call established who arranged external maintenance and when additional support might be needed.

Useful questions included:

  • Which equipment does your team maintain, and where do you use outside contractors?
  • Are any shutdowns or larger maintenance jobs planned?
  • Is there work your internal team struggles to cover with current staffing?
  • Are you adding or relocating production equipment?
  • How do you approve a new service provider?

We called in local business hours and used the contact’s preferred callback time when they were on the production floor. For an agreed follow-up, the notes identified the equipment, planned work and reason to call again.

An active breakdown was referred for a direct technical response. It was not treated as a routine prospecting meeting.

What we changed after the first calls

Week 1: Find the person booking contractors. Some plant managers referred us straight to maintenance. We changed the routing question to “Who coordinates outside contractors for planned maintenance?” and added the correct contact to the site record.

Weeks 2–3: Explain where the provider could help. A broad introduction about maintenance services often led to a quick answer about the internal team. We made the purpose more specific: extra mechanical support for shutdowns, larger jobs and work outside the team’s available capacity.

Month 2: Check equipment fit earlier. We moved the equipment and work-scope questions ahead of the meeting request. That gave the provider’s technical team enough information to check whether the opportunity belonged with them.

Month 3: Work from the shutdown calendar. When a contact mentioned a future shutdown, we recorded when contractors would be selected. Follow-up was scheduled before that decision, with a reminder of the work discussed.

Weekly reviews covered the quality of the handoff: whether the right person was involved, the service scope was clear and the proposed work fit the territory and technical capabilities. Those reviews fed back into the calling questions.

How we handled the common objections

“We have our own maintenance team.” We asked whether they used external technicians for shutdowns, specialist repairs or larger jobs. If everything was covered and no additional work was planned, we closed the record.

“We already have a contractor.” We checked whether the site ever needed additional capacity or considered another approved supplier. A meeting only made sense if the contact had a reason to explore that option.

“Nothing is coming up.” We asked when maintenance plans would next be reviewed. Where the contact agreed to reconnect around that date, we created a follow-up task.

“Send your capabilities.” We asked which equipment or service area they wanted covered. Sending the document alone did not count as a qualified opportunity.

What the sales team received

Before booking, we confirmed that the site was within the service area, the work appeared to fit the provider’s capabilities, and the contact had a specific reason to meet. The agreed next conversation could cover a planned job, an upcoming shutdown or additional supplier capacity.

The brief recorded what was known and what still required technical confirmation.

Example meeting brief

  • Site: Example site 027
  • Business: US packaging manufacturer operating multiple production shifts
  • Contact: Maintenance Manager
  • Current setup: Internal maintenance team, with outside contractors used for larger planned jobs
  • Reason for interest: An upcoming shutdown includes conveyor repairs and mechanical work the internal team may need help completing
  • Purpose of the meeting: Discuss the equipment, expected scope, shutdown window and availability of external technicians
  • Supplier process: Maintenance recommends contractors; purchasing handles approval
  • Still to confirm: Equipment details, final dates, site requirements, budget and contractor onboarding
  • Status: Booked — attendance pending

Following up before the work was assigned

The 34 follow-up sites had a relevant future need and agreed to further contact, but had not booked a meeting by campaign end. Examples included a shutdown with dates still being finalized, an expansion awaiting approval or a supplier review scheduled for later.

Each task named the contact, the planned work and the next date to reconnect. That let the next call pick up from the maintenance plan already discussed.

Bookings ranged from 10 to 21 per month. The campaign handoff gave the provider a clear basis for its next conversation: the site, equipment, likely work and timing.
