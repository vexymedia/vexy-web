Finding the companies whose systems were falling behind their operations

An ERP conversation rarely starts with a company asking for a new system. It starts with something the current setup makes difficult: reconciling stock across warehouses, planning production in spreadsheets, or pulling reports from several systems.

This example follows a phone-led campaign for a US ERP provider serving midsize manufacturers and wholesale distributors. We looked for a specific reason to talk, then booked an initial discussion with the person responsible for that part of the business.

We started with the processes the provider could actually support

Before calling, we worked through the provider’s fit criteria: manufacturing and distribution workflows, supported integrations, implementation requirements and the situations its sales team wanted to discuss.

The list focused on US companies with 50–500 employees. We checked company websites, business profiles, location pages and public expansion announcements. Useful clues included a second warehouse, a new production facility or additional operating locations. Each clue became a question to verify on the call.

We kept manufacturers and distributors in separate calling lists. Manufacturers were asked about production planning, materials and shop-floor reporting. Distributors were asked about inventory visibility, purchasing and orders moving between locations.

Depending on the company, the first useful contact was a CFO, operations leader, IT director or owner. We also recorded who would need to join a later ERP evaluation.

What happened on the phone

We called during the prospect’s local business hours. If the person was unavailable, we recorded a callback time or asked who owned the relevant process. Requested information went out by email with a short recap of the conversation.

Once we reached the right person, the questions stayed close to their daily work:

  • Which system handles finance, inventory and production today?
  • Where does the team still use spreadsheets or enter the same information twice?
  • Can people see reliable stock and order information across locations?
  • Has growth created work the current system struggles to handle?
  • Is a review planned, and who would be involved?

A company satisfied with its setup and without a planned change was closed out. A company describing a specific issue stayed in the conversation, even if a full ERP replacement was still some way off.

The changes we made during the campaign

Weeks 1–2: Make the opening question useful. Asking which ERP a company used often produced a product name and little else. We moved to a process question: “How are you handling production planning outside the ERP today?” For distributors, the equivalent question focused on stock across locations. The existing system was recorded after we understood the workflow.

Weeks 3–4: Reach the owner of the problem. IT contacts could describe the software but sometimes referred reporting or inventory issues elsewhere. We added finance and operations contacts to those accounts and recorded the referral, so the next caller had a specific person and reason to ask for.

Month 2: Separate a problem from a project. A spreadsheet workaround alone did not qualify a meeting. We added questions about its effect on the business, whether anyone was reviewing it and what the prospect wanted from a first discussion.

Month 3: Give follow-up a date and a reason. Accounts waiting for a planning cycle or internal review received a callback linked to that event. We reopened the conversation from the previous notes rather than repeating the introduction.

Each week, we reviewed call notes and handoff feedback with the provider’s sales team. Questions about deployment, migration and integrations were passed to its specialists; the caller’s job was to establish enough context for that discussion.

How we handled the usual objections

“We already have an ERP.” We asked which processes still happened outside it and whether that arrangement was causing any problems. If the answer was no, there was no reason to push a meeting.

“Replacing it would be a huge project.” We acknowledged the work involved and checked whether the company was considering a review at all. The first meeting could establish requirements and timing without committing the prospect to a migration.

“Send something over.” We asked what would be useful: information about manufacturing workflows, integrations or implementation. An email request on its own stayed out of the qualified totals.

What qualified a booked meeting

The account had to fit the provider’s customer profile, have a specific issue or planned review, and include someone who owned or could influence the project. The prospect also had to agree to a defined next conversation and a time.

Budget, implementation scope and purchase authority could still need confirmation. Those gaps were visible in the handoff.

Example meeting brief

  • Account: Example account 014
  • Business: US manufacturer with approximately 180 employees and two operating locations
  • Contact: Director of Operations
  • Current setup: On-premise ERP, with production scheduling and some inventory reconciliation handled in spreadsheets
  • Reason for interest: A second location has made it harder to keep production plans and inventory information aligned
  • Purpose of the meeting: Review the workflow and discuss whether the provider can support production planning and multi-location inventory
  • Other stakeholders: Finance and IT would need to join a later evaluation
  • Still to confirm: Budget, integration requirements, project owner and implementation timing
  • Status: Booked — attendance pending

The 41 accounts that needed more time

These companies had a relevant issue and agreed to another conversation, but had not booked a meeting by campaign end. Reasons included a later budgeting cycle, an internal systems review or a decision to involve another stakeholder first.

Each record included the issue, the person to contact and the agreed next step. A general request for a brochure did not qualify for this group.

Across the three months, bookings ranged from 18 to 24 per month. The handoff gave the sales team a starting point: the current setup, the operational issue and what the prospect wanted to discuss.
